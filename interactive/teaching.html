5-minute demonstration

Open the projector presentation

  1. 0:00–0:40: Show how the source drive becomes a fog candidate. Introduce test-time scaling.
  2. 0:40–1:20: Vote on 2 takes. Ask for one visual reason.
  3. 1:20–2:10: Increase the pool to 8. Switch the rubric, then apply a street-fidelity gate of 97.
  4. 2:10–3:15: Play the paired-review animation. Open the frost case and ask why the recorded structure rating stayed at 5/5.
  5. 3:15–5:00: Choose a final scene, reveal the edit, and name a reason to keep or reject it.
15–20-minute self-guided route
  1. 4 minutes: opening diagram, fog vote, and sample-budget comparison.
  2. 3 minutes: adjust the illustrative rubric and try the street-fidelity threshold of 97 to retain none.
  3. 3 minutes: inspect flooded mud, the rail yard, and one evidence comparison.
  4. 5 minutes: assess both judge cases, reveal recorded responses, and apply explicit rules.
  5. 2–5 minutes: complete the worksheet or explore the model and throughput notes.

These are suggested timings. Beginners may need longer.

35–40-minute classroom route
  1. 0–7 minutes: establish inference, conditioning, random seed, and Best-of-N. Learners record independent observations on the fog pair.
  2. 7–14 minutes: compare N and rubric priorities. Demonstrate a threshold that excludes every candidate.
  3. 14–22 minutes: examine the transformations and compare evidence from real snow or the prompt test.
  4. 22–30 minutes: predict the judge's behavior. Inspect the full recorded responses and discuss the 735/735 structure-score ceiling.
  5. 30–40 minutes: complete the worksheet in pairs, exchange critiques, and identify a held-out test for the proposed selector.
Facilitation, misconceptions, and accessibility
  • Before teaching: test the videos and full-screen controls, choose a route, and keep the extracted site available offline.
  • More samples: distinguish the best available candidate, the highest proxy score, and independently assessed usefulness.
  • Judge authority: a fluent description or a score of 5 is evidence to inspect. The hood case makes the inconsistency concrete.
  • Dataset use: the study does not demonstrate improved downstream perception performance or preserved ground-truth labels.
  • Accessible participation: use the motion pause control, keyboard buttons, the descriptive labels, and recorded textual responses. Video descriptions support discussion but are not independent visual validation.
  • Assessment: reward a justified decision and a clear evidence boundary. Do not require agreement with a predetermined favorite clip.

Design a verifier for another task.

Choose a task involving generated outputs. State what would count as useful, what would force rejection, and how you would evaluate the verifier itself.

A strong answer connects the decision rule to observable evidence and identifies a realistic way it could fail.

Instructor assessment guide

Use 0, 1, or 2 points per worksheet item: 0 for missing or unsupported reasoning; 1 for a plausible answer with an important gap; 2 for a clear requirement, relevant evidence, and an appropriate limitation. Total: 10 points.

For the nighttime example, a strong response distinguishes darkness and light-source behavior from generic wet-road realism. For the hood example, it identifies an unrequested foreground change despite the high score. For the real-snow comparison, it accounts for different traffic and viewpoints across days.

These are assessment anchors, not validated model scores. Accept other well-supported interpretations.
